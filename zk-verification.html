thesis 2 · the evidence

Enforce in real time. Prove it after the fact. Say exactly what the proof means.

runtime1's verification pipeline splits agent governance into two halves with very different physics: enforcement, which agentd does natively and instantaneously, and evidence, which must be cryptographic and checkable by someone who doesn't trust you. Confusing the two is how this space overclaims — so we don't.

The two-pipeline architecture

Proving a policy decision in a zkVM takes seconds; an agent runtime makes thousands of tool and model calls a day. Putting cryptography in the authorization path would be a denial-of-service against the runtime itself. So the pipelines are separated — agentd enforces, and the verification pipeline proves: 

  agentd ENFORCEMENT (hot path, microseconds)            EVIDENCE PIPELINE (post-hoc, crypto)
  ┌───────────────────────────────────────┐            ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ agent requests action                 │            │ every decision → append-only log    │
  │   → capability check                  │  journals  │   Ed25519 hash chain, rotated       │
  │   → budget admission (rolling window) │ ─────────▶ │   segments, offline verifier        │
  │   → approval gate (if risky)          │            │ + witness cosignature, independent  │
  │   → credential broker executes        │            │ + zkVM interval proofs (private)    │
  │ allow / deny — in real time           │            │ verified in milliseconds, by others │
  └───────────────────────────────────────┘            └─────────────────────────────────────┘

The prover never sits in front of an action, never lives inside agentd (the ~2 MB PID-1 runtime), and never becomes a reason an agent can't act. Enforcement is code that already runs; evidence is a record that already exists, progressively hardened until a stranger can check it.

What exists today shipped

  • The signed receipt chain. Model calls — allowed and denied — are receipted into evidence.jsonl: hash-chained, Ed25519-signed, rotated in independently verifiable segments, resumable after crashes.
  • An offline verifier. agentctl verify checks a segment against a public key with no runtime, no network, no trust in the box that produced it.
  • The complete action record. Every tool call, capability verdict, approval, cancel, and budget decision lands in the flight recorder — the raw material the evidence pipeline progressively signs.
  • A signed decision journal, in production. Every admission decision — allow, defer, deny — is a canonical record with its inputs declared, in an append-only Ed25519-signed hash chain. Recording against a real inbox, not a fixture.
  • Replay, inclusion proofs, and a verifier the buyer runs. agentctl replay re-derives every recorded verdict from the journal's own hash-verified inputs; agentctl prove emits a Merkle path checkable offline with nothing but a public key; agentos-verifier is a separate narrow binary with no scheduler and no network runtime.
  • An independent witness. A second party, holding its own key, replays the leaves since the head it last saw and cosigns only if the new head append-only-extends it — the first check here that does not rest on a key the audited party holds.
  • A zero-knowledge proof over real records. A zkVM guest proves an interval is contiguous, chain-linked, input-bound and verdict-consistent, with the records as private witness. It has run over the production journal; the timings are published.
  • A public account of the limits. Our threat model states, in writing, what this chain does not prove: the signing key lives on the audited host, and deletion seams between rotated segments are not detectable from the chain alone. It is the most load-bearing paragraph in the repo, and it is written down rather than glossed over.

The honest core: what evidence can and cannot say

what cryptography can prove

Integrity: the published log was not modified or reordered after writing.

Rewind detection (with anchoring): records that were journaled and anchored cannot be silently deleted or rewritten later.

Policy consistency (with zk replay): every decision in the published log re-evaluates to the same verdict under the committed policy — without revealing the policy.

what no signature or proof can say

Completeness. An action that was never journaled produces no gap. Cryptography over a log inherits the log; "every action was recorded" is a property of the boundary, not of any proof over its output.

Closing that seam structurally takes hardware attestation with credentials sealed inside the attested boundary — sketched in the roadmap for multi-machine setups, and scoped honestly to brokered actions if it ever lands.

Our claim discipline is binding across docs, demos, and this site: we say "policy-consistency of the published, custody-anchored log" — never "proof of enforcement," never "mathematical certainty that all policies were enforced." A proof artifact that looks like certainty while quietly carrying an omission seam is worse than no cryptography at all, because it teaches your auditor to stop asking the right question.

The verification ladder

Each rung is a separate, independently shippable increment. Order matters: the proof lands on top of auditor-grade evidence, never ahead of it. Ten rungs are built; every one of them is checkable by running something rather than by reading this page.

Why zero-knowledge, specifically

One property in this space is unique to zk and impossible any other way: a verifier who must check policy compliance without ever seeing the policy. The rules themselves — which senders can trigger actions, what spend is normal, which systems an agent may touch — can be sensitive. The zk replay proof lets an outside party confirm that a set of published decision logs is consistent with the committed policy while learning nothing about the rules or payloads beyond the commitments themselves.

The boundary of that story matters just as much, and one axis of it flipped during the build. The original design put records public and the policy private; the shipped one is the other way round, because the runtime already publishes its ceilings and a budget is recoverable by binary-searching the verdict flip regardless. So the honest statement is records private, policy public: the interval's records are the private witness, and what goes public is two MMR roots, a four-way tally and the identity fields. When the verifier is the policy owner, native replay still gives the same assurance for free — the proof earns its keep when the records themselves must not be shown. And a proof over a log inherits the log's completeness limits, which is why the ladder puts coverage, custody, and the trust root first. The economics hold because proofs are batched over an interval rather than per action: measured on this project's own hardware, seconds to prove and ~0.17 s to verify, with verification time flat across interval sizes.

design rules the ladder cannot break

1 — The prover is a separate binary. zkVM dependency trees never enter agentd or its PID-1 boot path; the ~2 MB footprint and its CI guard are untouched.

2 — One compiled policy artifact serves enforcement and proving. Dual compilation of "the same" policy is how the proven policy silently drifts from the enforced one.

3 — A missing proof is loud. Prover down means a visible, signed gap record in the packet — enforcement is native and unaffected, and an outage is never silently absorbed.

4 — Every claim names its seam. Integrity, rewind detection, and policy consistency are provable; completeness is a boundary property; we label which is which everywhere.

The verifier's experience, end state

Whoever is checking — the operator, or someone auditing on their behalf — gets an audit packet per agent per period: chain segments, anchors, the decision journal or its commitments, and — where the zk rungs have landed — replay proofs. Verification is offline and fast: check the chain, check the anchors against the registry, check the proof. Milliseconds per packet, no trust in the host that produced it, and a written statement of exactly which properties were just verified.

That last artifact — the written statement of what was verified — is the whole point. Novel cryptography is easy to be impressed by and hard to rely on; a narrow statement you can actually defend is worth more.
